Is Real Madrid special? Testing the claim that VAR works against Real Madrid with nine seasons of La Liga data

Data mining, cleaning, validation, statistical framework and results
Draft for review · 12 October 2026 · Revised 15 October 2026 (pipeline audit added as Section 4.5 and Appendix D; no estimate changed) · Scope: La Liga, 2018-19 (VAR introduced) to 12 October 2026 (2026-27 partial, 8 rounds) · Code and data: github.com/RoloMagnus/Real_Madrid_VAR_null_goals_outlier_study

Abstract. On 10 October 2026 a widely shared post listed the La Liga teams with the most goals ruled out by VAR since 2018; Real Madrid led with 42 (+1 that day), nearly twice the runner-up. Fans read this as evidence of referee or VAR bias against the club. We (i) rebuilt the underlying event data from four public sources covering all 3,118 league matches played since VAR was introduced, (ii) validated the post and our own reconstruction against independent providers and a manual video check, and (iii) tested whether the excess is explained by how much and how Real Madrid attacks, or whether it carries the fingerprints of unequal treatment.

The outlier is real but the post's exact figures are not reproducible: we find 50 VAR-nullified goals for Real Madrid versus 30 for the next team (Villarreal), a rate of 0.160 per match against 0.058 for all other teams (rate ratio 2.8, 95% CI 2.0–3.7). Attack volume, offside volume, pressing, fouls and cards together explain about half of the count (a model fitted on the other teams predicts ~25 goals, 95% interval 16–35); a residual rate ratio of about 2.0 remains. However, the residual shows none of the signatures of targeting: within the same matches the split of nullified goals follows each side's share of offsides and goal opportunities, opponents are not significantly spared, Real Madrid's rate does not differ across 15 referees, the same excess appears in goals ruled out on the pitch without any VAR review, and published VAR accuracy (96–98%) is far too high for errors to account for it. Counting every Real Madrid nullified goal as wrong would change one league title (2020-21). The unexplained residual is most consistent with Real Madrid's attacking profile, a hypothesis that requires offside-line tracking data to confirm.

Headline results
  • 50 VAR-nullified Real Madrid goals in 44 of 312 league matches; 15 of those matches ended in a draw or loss and in 13 the result would have changed (21 points over 8.2 seasons).
  • Rate ratio versus other teams: 2.76 raw → 2.01 after controlling for offsides, shots, xG, home and season (top-10 teams only: 1.84); 1.99 with pressing (PPDA) added; 2.02 with fouls and cards added.
  • No significant within-match asymmetry, no referee concentration, no evidence that opponents are protected; on-field (non-VAR) disallowed goals show the same direction (rate ratio 2.0); the extra Real Madrid effect at the VAR stage is 1.36× (95% CI 0.82–2.23, p = 0.23).
  • Title counterfactual (all 50 goals counted): 1 of 5 non-title seasons flips (2020-21); at a realistic 1.7–5% VAR error rate the chance is 1.9%–5.1%.

1. Background and research question

The Video Assistant Referee (VAR) was introduced in La Liga in the 2018-19 season. On 10 October 2026 the analyst MisterChip (@2010MisterChip) published a table titled "Equipos con más goles anulados por VAR desde que se usa el videoarbitraje en La Liga (2018-2026)": Real Madrid 42 (+1 that day), Atlético de Madrid 22, Villarreal 22, Celta 20, Real Sociedad 20, Sevilla 20 and Barcelona 19. The post reached 1.1 million views and prompted claims of referee influence and institutional corruption against Real Madrid.

A ranking of raw counts cannot support such claims by itself. Counts depend on exposure (how often a team attacks and scores), on the way it attacks (a team that plays runners behind a high line produces more offside goals), on the data source, and on chance. We therefore ask four questions:

  1. Validation. Are the figures in the post correct?
  2. Description. For each Real Madrid goal ruled out after VAR review: when, against whom, by which player, why, under which referee and at what position in the table?
  3. Inference. Is Real Madrid's count an outlier after accounting for attacking volume and style, and, if so, does the residual carry the fingerprints of targeting (against Real Madrid) or favouritism (towards its opponents)?
  4. Impact. Did these decisions cost Real Madrid points, and would any of them have changed a league title?

1.1 Hypotheses

  • H0 (neutral officiating). A team's nullified goals are Poisson-distributed with a rate depending only on its attacking exposure; Real Madrid has no special effect.
  • H1a (targeting or favouritism). Real Madrid's rate exceeds what exposure implies and the excess appears within matches (same referee, same VAR team) as opponents being spared, and/or concentrates in specific referees, and/or is specific to the VAR stage.
  • H1b (profile). Real Madrid's rate exceeds what simple exposure implies, but the excess is symmetric within matches, spread over referees and equally present in decisions taken without VAR: a property of how the team attacks rather than of how it is treated.

The analysis is observational. It can reject specific, observable consequences of H1a; it cannot prove the absence of bias.

2. Data collection

No official VAR log exists in the public domain. Event data were therefore assembled from four public sources, each used for what it does best, with deliberate redundancy so that no conclusion depends on a single provider (Figure 1).

2.1 Acquisition

ESPN. The scoreboard endpoint accepts a single day or a calendar year but rejects date ranges. Nine yearly requests (2018–2026) enumerate all fixtures, which were assigned to seasons by date (August–July, with the COVID-delayed 2019-20 finish in June–August 2020 mapped explicitly). Each match summary was then downloaded concurrently (12 threads, 4 retries, on-disk cache): 3,210 summaries, of which 3,112 were completed matches, 97 scheduled fixtures and one abandoned match.

Flashscore. Each season's results page embeds the first 25 matches; further pages are available from a paginated feed keyed by a tournament-season code, which we discovered by inspecting the page's JavaScript and scanning candidate codes. For each of the 3,118 completed matches we downloaded four feeds (incidents, statistics, match information with the referee, and live commentary). Commentary for every match was downloaded because the incident feed alone omits goals chalked off by on-field officials. Season coverage is complete: 380 matches in each of the eight full seasons plus 78 in 2026-27.

Transfermarkt and Understat. Matchday pages were scraped to map each fixture to its match-sheet identifier (3,109 of 3,118 matched after an alias fix for Athletic Club/Athletic Bilbao), then the sheets needed for the analysis were fetched. Understat's league endpoint returned team-match xG and PPDA for every season.

Sources that failed. Sofascore returned HTTP 403 for scripted requests and a search engine returned a captcha; both were abandoned rather than circumvented. ESPN's referee field was populated for only 116 matches and was therefore not used.

Terms of use. The endpoints are undocumented and unofficial; the terms of the providers apply. The repository does not redistribute the raw caches (about 1.6 GB, regenerable by the scripts); it contains processed, derived tables.

3. Data cleaning and integration

3.1 Entity resolution

The same club appears under different names in each provider (e.g. Atl. Madrid, Atlético Madrid, Atlético de Madrid; Ath Bilbao, Athletic Club; Celta Vigo, Celta de Vigo). Names were normalised by stripping diacritics, lower-casing and token-set matching, with a small explicit alias table for the residual cases. Players are given as Mbappe K. in Flashscore; we reconstructed full accented names by converting Flashscore's URL slug (mbappe-kylian) to a token set and looking it up in ESPN's roster names (Kylian Mbappé).

3.2 Time, seasons and match keys

Kick-off times were converted from UTC to Europe/Madrid. ESPN and Flashscore matches were joined on (kick-off hour, home goals, away goals), with ambiguous joins dropped rather than guessed; Flashscore's own identifier is the primary key of the panel. A post-draft audit (Section 4.5) found that the foul-zone join, which used a looser team-name match, attached ESPN's La Coruña–Valencia match to the Alavés–Valencia fixture of September 2026 for the foul-zone covariates; this affects one match and no result.

3.3 Defining the outcome: a VAR-nullified goal

A goal is VAR-nullified if it was scored, then ruled out after a VAR review. Flashscore's incident feed logs 397 Goal Disallowed events league-wide (offside 281, foul 50, handball 49, unspecified video review 17). An event is VAR-confirmed if either (a) Flashscore commentary within −1/+3 minutes carries VAR wording (the explicit var icon, or text such as "VAR review", "video assistant referee"), or (b) ESPN logs an overturned goal, a VAR "no goal" decision or a "Video Review" delay within three minutes. This gave 394 VAR-confirmed events; three events with no VAR evidence (none involving Real Madrid) were excluded from all tests.

Events were then classified: by reason (offside, attacking foul, handball, unspecified) and, for offsides, by who was offside using ESPN's typed offside events (a matching event within ±1 minute for the same team): goalscorer (125 of 397 events), teammate in the build-up (101) or not identified (57). The latter is how the "passive player" field of the report is filled. The half is derived from the base minute (stoppage time inherits the half). The context of each goal (score before the goal, what the goal would have meant, final result, points that would have been gained) is computed from the incident feed's running score.

A boundary case that shaped the definition. During manual review a second Mbappé goal in the 0–4 Clásico of 26 October 2024 (65') was proposed. It is genuine (“The assistant referee raises his flag and the referee blows his whistle. Mbappé puts the ball into the net, but he is flagged for offside”) but involved no VAR review in either feed. It was therefore not a VAR-nullified goal. This motivated a separate dataset of goals ruled out on the pitch (Section 3.7) and a test of whether the VAR stage differs from the on-field stage (Section 6.6).

3.4 Referees

Full referee names come from Transfermarkt match sheets for all 617 relevant matches (100% resolved for matches with a nullified goal); Flashscore's abbreviated names were mapped to full names by majority vote over paired matches. There are 41 distinct referees. The referee recorded is the on-field referee, the formal decision-maker after VAR advice; the VAR-room official is not published consistently by any accessible source.

3.5 League position at the time of each goal

No provider supplies the table at kick-off, so every season was rebuilt from results. For the panel, the table before each kick-off is sorted by points, goal difference, goals scored. Round-1 events have no table and are left blank (7 events). For the title scenarios (Section 6.8) the full La Liga tie-break order is applied (points, head-to-head points, head-to-head goal difference, overall goal difference, goals scored). The reconstruction was checked against known outcomes: the champion and top four of each season match the earlier table, and champions' points match the official totals (e.g. Barcelona 87, Real Madrid 87, Atlético 86, Real Madrid 86, Barcelona 88, Real Madrid 95, Barcelona 88 and 94).

3.6 Exposure and covariate construction

  • Shots, shots on target, offsides, corners, fouls, cards, possession from Flashscore match statistics. Red cards are not listed when zero (79% blanks) and were set to zero.
  • xG and PPDA from Understat, joined by team and date (±2 days). Flashscore xG exists only from 2022-23 and was used as a fallback and as a cross-check. 1.2% of team-matches lack xG and 1.9% lack PPDA, almost entirely Huesca and Oviedo, whose names differ between Flashscore and Understat (“Huesca” vs “SD Huesca”, “Oviedo” vs “Real Oviedo”; see Section 4.5); models are fitted on complete cases and the sample size is reported with each model. PPDA is passes allowed per defensive action; the opponent's PPDA in the same match is taken from the other team's row.
  • Fouls by zone. ESPN records a foul as two lines: Foul by X (Team) and Y (Team) wins a free kick in the defensive half / attacking half / on the left (right) wing. The location is stated from the fouled player's perspective, so a free kick won "in the defensive half" is a foul committed in the committer's attacking half. We verified the orientation against player roles (forwards generate that tag 78% of the time, defenders about 44%). Penalty-area fouls are logged only as Penalty conceded by… and were parsed separately. ESPN captures about 91% of box-score fouls, so zone counts are used as shares and as covariates, not as totals.
  • Top-10 scope. At the request of the study sponsor, comparisons are also reported for the ten best teams of each season's final table, which removes relegated teams by construction (see threat 3 in Section 8).

3.7 Goals ruled out on the pitch

Mining the commentary of all matches for five recurring phrasings (“puts the ball into the net, but he is flagged for offside”; “A goal, which was scored by…, was disallowed due to…”; “Goal disallowed! X scores but the referee blows his whistle for a foul”; “immediately ruled out by referee… due to a foul/handball”; “thought he had scored but it was disallowed…”) and removing lines mentioning VAR, events within four minutes of a VAR-confirmed event for the same team, and duplicate descriptions of the same goal yields 298 on-field disallowed goals (Real Madrid 29). Because it relies on phrasing, this count is a floor.

3.8 Resulting analysis panel

The analysis panel has 6,236 team-matches (3,118 matches × 2), each with exposure covariates, table position, VAR-nullified and on-field disallowed counts, and the season's final position. The Real Madrid breakdown has 50 rows (one per goal) with 28 fields.

4. Validation

4.1 Reconstructing the post

The post's figures are lower than our reconstruction for every team listed (Table 2, Figure 2). Real Madrid is first in both, and the ranking of the top is similar, but the post's runner-up gap (42 vs 22, 1.9×) is wider than ours (50 vs 30, 1.7×), and Barcelona is third in our data but seventh in the post. Restricting to offside and attacking-foul reasons reproduces Real Madrid (42), Atlético (22) and Celta (20) exactly but not the other teams, so no single definition reconciles the post. We conclude that the post's provider and definition are unknown, that its exact numbers should not be relied upon, and that its direction (Real Madrid first, by a wide margin) is confirmed.

4.4 Manual validation

A hand-built sample of four rows, prepared by the study team independently of the pipeline, was checked against the raw feeds. The errors it exposed were errors of the hand-built sample, not of the pipeline output: one wrong minute (18', not 43'), one match with no VAR review at all (Valencia, 1 December 2018), and one row that merged two matches (the Modrić/Varane offside belongs to the 16 March 2019 match against Celta, not the Valladolid match). The pipeline already held the correct record for each: Real Madrid–Levante at 18'; no incident at all for Valencia on 1 December 2018; Modrić's goal in the Celta match; and only Valladolid's own goal in the Valladolid match. Such errors show how bad state can enter a manually assembled table, which is why Section 4.5 audits the pipeline itself. All 50 Real Madrid goals were then reviewed by the study team against match video. The final breakdown matches the manually validated list one-to-one (50 of 50, matched on date, player and minute) and carries the validation flag and video link of each goal.

4.5 Pipeline audit (added after publication of the first draft)

A reader observed that a clean pipeline can still carry bad state all the way through, citing the kinds of error found in the hand-built sample: a wrong minute, a non-existent VAR review and merged matches. We therefore tested each type directly against the raw feeds for the whole dataset (scripts in scripts/audit/; full write-up in AUDIT.md; summary in Appendix D).

  • Minutes. Flashscore's incident minute is the minute of play in which the goal was scored (1-indexed; a goal at clock time 53:43 is the 54th minute). ESPN stamps the VAR outcome at decision time. For the 286 events that ESPN tags explicitly, the two minutes are identical in 47% of cases, within one minute in 79% and within two in 95.5% (all found inside a ±3-minute search window); Flashscore is earlier in 136 events because the decision follows the review. Differences of one minute against other sources are a rounding convention, not an error.
  • VAR reviews that never happened. An independent re-derivation of the evidence behind all 397 candidate incidents found explicit VAR wording and/or an explicit ESPN VAR event for 391 (286 have both). Of the other six, three were excluded; two are counted on an ESPN “Video Review” delay alone (Real Madrid–Levante 2018, validated against video by the study team, and Valencia–Girona 2018); and one is counted although its commentary never mentions VAR (Getafe, July 2020, not a Real Madrid goal), because the matching rule accepted the bare word “disallow”, which also describes on-field decisions. In a stricter dataset that keeps only explicit VAR wording (391 events, Real Madrid 49) the rate ratio is 2.72 (95% CI 1.97–3.68) against 2.76 in the main analysis. In all 389 events with VAR commentary the VAR lines refer to the goal (none concerns only a penalty or a card), no incident is counted twice, and 122 of the 128 goals ESPN labels as overturned by VAR from 2022-23 onwards are in our list (none of the six missing involves Real Madrid).
  • Merged or mismatched matches. In the main pipeline: none. For the 3,034 matches with exactly one ESPN candidate under the kick-off-hour-plus-score key, team names agree in all 3,034; the 78 ambiguous matches are left blank by design (18 events, none Real Madrid). In all 6,120 team-matches joined to Understat the goals scored and conceded equal the Flashscore score, and Transfermarkt referee surnames agree with Flashscore in 616 of 617 matches (one 2026-27 disagreement, not a Real Madrid match). Two defects were found in secondary covariates: the foul-zone script mapped ESPN's “Deportivo” (La Coruña) to Deportivo Alavés, so one fixture received another match's foul data and seven La Coruña matches have none; and the Huesca and Oviedo aliases were missing, which removed 76 team-matches from every xG model and 230 from the PPDA models.

In total the audit found five small defects (Appendix D, Table 24). None changes Real Madrid's count of 50, and re-running the analysis with the defects fixed in an isolated copy moves no estimate by more than 0.05 on a rate ratio of about 2.0 (Table 25). We left the published estimates unchanged and document the fixes. A more fundamental limitation remains: all sources are unofficial public feeds and Flashscore and ESPN are not guaranteed to be independent of each other (both may derive from the same upstream provider), so their agreement is weaker evidence than independent provenance.

5. Methodology

5.1 Unit of analysis and exposure

The unit is the team-match. The outcome is the number of VAR-nullified goals a team scored in that match. Counts are compared against several exposures because no single one is adequate: matches played, shots, shots on target, xG, offside flags (the offside-goal opportunity), corners and goal events (goals plus nullified goals). Using several exposures protects against conclusions that depend on the choice of denominator.

5.2 Statistical tests

TestQuestionMethod
A. Rate ratiosIs Real Madrid's rate higher?Exact conditional Poisson test (binomial given the total) for the ratio of two rates, with exact CI; run for each exposure and for two scopes (all 20 teams; top-10 of the final table).
B. Poisson regressionHow much of the effect do exposure variables absorb?GLM with log link, Real Madrid indicator plus log(offsides+1), log(shots+1), xG, home and season fixed effects; standard errors clustered by match; Pearson dispersion reported (1.03–1.11, so Poisson is adequate). Extended with PPDA (own and opponent, att and def, logged) and with fouls, attacking-half fouls, yellow and red cards, always on a common complete-case sample so Real Madrid coefficients are comparable across specifications.
C. Leave-Real-Madrid-out forecastHow many would Real Madrid have if it behaved like the others?Fit models on the other teams only, predict Real Madrid's 312 matches, simulate the total with gamma-Poisson draws (20,000) when dispersion > 1, report the 95% interval and the probability of observing at least 50.
D. Within-match symmetryIn the same matches (same referee, same VAR room, same pitch and day), is Real Madrid's share of the nullified goals what its attack share predicts?Conditional on the match total, simulate the Real Madrid count as binomial with probability equal to Real Madrid's share of offsides, shots, xG or goal opportunities in that match (20,000 draws). A bias against Real Madrid would reject the offside and goal-opportunity nulls.
E. FavouritismAre Real Madrid's opponents spared?Compare nullified goals for opponents in matches against Real Madrid with their own season rates (per match, per offside, per shot); Poisson test for fewer than expected.
F. Referee heterogeneityIs the effect concentrated in particular referees?Chi-square test of homogeneity of Real Madrid's rate across the 15 referees with at least 8 Real Madrid matches; comparison with the same referee's rate for other teams.
G. VAR vs on-field stageIs the Real Madrid excess specific to VAR?Rate tests and Poisson models on on-field disallowed goals, and a stacked model with a Real Madrid × stage interaction.
H. CorrelationsDo attack volume and offsides track nullified goals across top-10 team-seasons?Pearson, Spearman and Kendall coefficients with 2,000-sample bootstrap CIs; with and without Real Madrid; at team-season and team-aggregate level.
I. Title scenariosWould the nullified goals have changed any title?Rebuild each season from all 380 results, add back goals, recompute the whole table and re-rank with the La Liga tie-break; break-even analysis (dynamic programming over the matches) and a Monte-Carlo in which each nullification is wrong with probability p.

5.3 Counterfactual scenarios

  • A – Real Madrid only (VAR): add back all 50 Real Madrid VAR-nullified goals. This is an upper bound: it assumes every nullification was wrong.
  • B – Everyone (VAR): add back every team's VAR-nullified goals, i.e. a league in which VAR never nullifies a goal.
  • C – A plus on-field decisions: an extreme sensitivity that also treats goals ruled out on the pitch as valid.

When goals are added to a match, the opponent's points change accordingly, so the rival's total in the same match is recomputed.

5.4 Software

Python 3.12; pandas, numpy, scipy (exact tests, bootstrap), statsmodels (GLM with cluster-robust covariance), matplotlib/seaborn, xlsxwriter. All steps are scripted and seeded (seed 2026).

6. Results

6.1 Ranking and composition

Real Madrid has 50 VAR-nullified goals in 312 league matches, ahead of Villarreal (30), Barcelona (27), Atlético (26), Sevilla (25) and Celta (23) (Figure 3). 6.2 The excess is statistically unambiguous

Per match, Real Madrid's rate is 0.160 versus 0.058 for the other teams, a ratio of 2.76 (95% CI 2.01–3.72). The ratio shrinks when the rate is normalised by attacking exposure but is above 1.7 for every exposure we tried (Table 6; top-10 scope: 1.7–2.4). Because Real Madrid was singled out as the most extreme of 31 clubs, we checked that selection does not explain it: even a Bonferroni threshold across 31 teams (p < 0.0016) is met by a wide margin (exact one-sided p = 2.3e-09), and Real Madrid sits far above the 99.8% limit of a funnel plot of all teams (Figure 5). It is also not an artefact of a few multi-goal matches: 14.1% of Real Madrid's matches contain at least one nullified goal against 5.5% for other teams (odds ratio 2.8; the largest single-match count is 3).

6.3 How much does attacking volume explain?

Correlations (top-10 team-seasons). Across the top-10 team-seasons, nullified goals correlate moderately with chances created (shots r = 0.48, 95% CI 0.30–0.63; xG r = 0.49) and only weakly with offside flags (r = 0.21, p = 0.06). Excluding Real Madrid, the figures are 0.30 (shots), 0.38 (xG) and 0.20 (offsides): the association does not depend on Real Madrid alone, but Real Madrid sits well above the fitted line (Figure 6). Offside flags per match are close to the top-10 average (2.36 vs 2.14), so offside volume by itself does not single Real Madrid out.

Regression. In Poisson models Real Madrid's incident-rate ratio (IRR) falls from 2.74 with no controls to 2.01 after controlling for offsides, shots, xG, home and season (95% CI 1.45–2.79; top-10 scope 1.84, Table 7, Figure 7). Offside flags are the single strongest covariate (about 2.4 times more nullified goals per log-unit of offsides), but neither offsides nor shots nor xG absorbs the Real Madrid effect.

6.4 Pressing, fouls and cards

Real Madrid's profile differs from the other top-10 teams in several ways (Figure 9): more shots (1.31×), shots on target (1.40×), xG (1.32×), goals (1.33×), possession (1.14×) and corners (1.19×); about 1.30× more passes in the opponent's pressing zone; opponents press it much less (opponent PPDA 14.8 versus 11.1); and it commits fewer fouls (10.4 vs 12.9 per match; 5.7 vs 7.0 in the attacking half) and receives fewer yellow cards (1.88 vs 2.35); its own pressing intensity (PPDA 10.5 vs 10.0) and red cards (0.10 vs 0.11) are typical.

Adding own and opponent PPDA (attempts and defensive actions, logged) moves the Real Madrid IRR from 2.06 to 1.99 (6,006 team-matches; Table 9). Adding total fouls, attacking-half fouls, yellow and red cards moves it from 2.06 to 2.02 when PPDA is also included (5,980 team-matches). The model trained on other teams with all of these covariates expects ~25 goals (95% 16–36). Individual PPDA and discipline coefficients are imprecise (PPDA components are collinear by construction and most intervals include 1); the informative finding is the stability of the Real Madrid coefficient. The negative team-level correlation between fouls and nullified goals (r = −0.40 across top-10 team-seasons) is best read as possession-dominant teams fouling less and attacking more, not as discipline causing offsides.

6.5 Does the residual carry the fingerprints of targeting or favouritism?

Within-match symmetry (test D). If officials leaned against Real Madrid, then in Real Madrid matches (same referee, same VAR room) Real Madrid would lose more goals than its offside or goal-opportunity share predicts. It does not: Real Madrid has 50 of the 63 nullified goals in its matches (79%); its offside share predicts 46 (95% 41–51; p = 0.11) and its share of goal opportunities 46 (p = 0.14). Under shots and xG shares Real Madrid is above expectation (p = 0.002 and 0.014), which is the same signal as in the regressions: Real Madrid produces more offside goals than its shots imply, but not more than its offsides and goal opportunities imply.

6.6 Is the excess specific to VAR?

Goals ruled out by on-field officials without any VAR review show the same direction: Real Madrid has 29 of the 298 (rate ratio 2.0 raw, 1.4 after controls, p = 0.07), alongside Barcelona (28), Atlético (27) and Villarreal (26) (Figure 12). Combining both stages gives 79 Real Madrid events (2.4×). In a stacked model, the extra Real Madrid effect at the VAR stage compared with the on-field stage is 1.36× (95% CI 0.82–2.23, p = 0.23). The point estimate is above 1, so a modest VAR-specific effect cannot be excluded with 50 VAR events, but the data show no evidence of one: the elevated rate already exists before VAR is involved.

6.7 Descriptive profile of the 50 goals

Players. The goals involve 23 different scorers; 11 of them account for 75% of the incidents (76%; Figure 13). Karim Benzema and Kylian Mbappé lead with 8 each (32% together), followed by Marco Asensio and Vinícius Júnior with 4 each. Concentration is typical for a team of this size: the top-three share is 40% against a median of 38% for the other teams with at least 15 nullified goals (Figure 14). Normalising by offside flags, the conversion of a flag into a nullified goal is highest for Asensio and Valverde (small denominators) and about 7.7 per 100 flags for Mbappé, 4.5 for Benzema and 1.9 for Vinícius (Figure 15).

Timing and game state. Real Madrid's nullified goals are spread across the match (54% in the second half versus 53% for other teams); 22% fall in the first 15 minutes versus 13% for others, but with 11 events this is not significant (binomial p = 0.09). 23 of the 50 goals would have been openers and 6 equalisers, 19 would have extended a lead and 2 reduced a deficit (Figure 16).

Venue and opponent. Thirty of the 50 goals came at home against 20 away (0.192 vs 0.128 per match), but the difference is not significant (p = 0.20) and is present league-wide (other teams: 0.068 home vs 0.048 away). Against opponents outside the top 10 at kick-off Real Madrid's rate is 0.207 versus 0.113 against the top 10 (p = 0.05, borderline), and the same gradient exists for all teams (0.075 versus 0.051); deeper-defending sides concede more goals and more offside goals. None of these cuts is specific to Real Madrid.

Time. The count is irregular across seasons (Figure 17) and strongly clustered in two seasons (11 in 2022-23, 9 in 2024-25), which is why the champion-versus-not comparison below is imprecise.

6.8 Did it cost titles? Games affected, champion versus not, and scenarios

Matches directly affected. The 50 goals fall in 44 matches (14.1% of Real Madrid's league matches): 29 were wins (35 goals), 8 draws and 7 losses. Of the 15 matches that ended in a draw or loss, in 13 the result would have changed if every nullified goal had counted (8 draws would have been wins, 5 of the 7 losses draws), worth 21 points over 8.2 seasons (about 2.6 per season; Figure 18). Two losses (including the 0–4 Clásico) would not have changed.

Champion versus not champion. Real Madrid won three titles in the period (2019-20, 2021-22, 2023-24). In those seasons it had 13 nullified goals (4.3 per 38 matches; 26% of the total) against 34 in the five non-title seasons (6.8 per 38; 68%); the remaining 3 fall in the partial 2026-27 season. The rate is lower in title seasons but the difference is not significant (rate ratio 0.64, exact two-sided p = 0.18; Figure 19).

Title scenarios. Every season was rebuilt from all 380 results and re-ranked with the La Liga tie-break; the rebuilt actual tables reproduce the real champions in all eight full seasons. Counting all Real Madrid nullified goals as valid (scenario A, an upper bound) changes exactly one title: 2020-21, where Real Madrid (84) would finish level on 86 points with Atlético and ahead on head-to-head (4 points to 1); that requires one wrong call, a draw turned into a win. In 2022-23 Real Madrid would still finish one point behind Barcelona (85 vs 86), in 2024-25 two behind (86 vs 88), in 2025-26 six behind (88 vs 94) and in 2018-19 eighteen behind (Table 17, Figures 20–21). In the neutral scenario B (every team's nullified goals count), Real Madrid gains 2022-23 but loses 2023-24 (87 vs 88): the effects roughly offset. Only the extreme scenario C, which also reverses every on-field decision, would give Real Madrid six titles instead of three.

7. VAR accuracy in context

The brief for this study set a prior that VAR is more than 95% accurate and asked for La Liga evidence first, then the Premier League, then FIFA. We could not retrieve and verify a La Liga-specific accuracy publication. The evidence we could verify or locate is: (i) the peer-reviewed multi-league study of Spitz et al. (2021), covering 2,195 matches in 13 countries, with 9,732 VAR checks and 795 reviews, in which the accuracy of the final decision rose from 92.1% (initial referee call) to 98.3% after VAR (abstract verified via Europe PMC, PMID 32794432); (ii) FIFA's reported success rate of 99.3% at the 2018 World Cup, against 95% without VAR (as cited by Wikipedia); and (iii) headline-level reports that the Premier League claims 96% correct decisions (Sky Sports, February 2024) and a 98.9% figure in the IFAB experiment report (BBC, January 2018), and a claim by Spanish refereeing bodies of 98% accuracy on penalties (El País, December 2020). The last three were verified at headline level only. Spanish media have also published rankings of clubs “benefited and harmed” by VAR; their methods could not be retrieved and they were not used.

These accuracy figures matter in two ways. First, at 98.3% accuracy we would expect 0.9 wrong decisions among Real Madrid's 50 nullifications, 2.0 at 96% and 2.5 at 95%, versus an unexplained excess of about 25 goals; even a 20% error rate (more than twice the 7.9% error of unaided referees in Spitz et al.) would give only 10 wrong decisions. VAR error cannot be what produces the excess. Second, nullified offsides are geometric calls and the least subjective VAR category, and they dominate Real Madrid's count (72%). A bias story would have to operate through which incidents are flagged or reviewed, which the tests in Sections 6.5 and 6.6 do not support.

8. Discussion and threats to validity

8.1 Interpretation

The data support a clear, two-part reading. First, Real Madrid is a genuine statistical outlier: the excess is not noise, not a selection artefact, not driven by a few matches and not specific to one data provider. Second, the excess has the properties one would expect of an attacking-profile effect and lacks those that targeting would produce. It is roughly half explained by volume; the remainder is present to a similar degree in decisions taken without VAR; it is symmetric within matches when conditioned on offsides and goal opportunities; it is spread over referees; and opponents are not protected. A plausible mechanism, consistent with the profile data (1.3× shots, 1.3× passes in the opponent's pressing zone, opponents pressing Real Madrid far less) and with the dominance of offside decisions, is that Real Madrid's attackers receive the ball in behind more often and from more advanced positions, creating more goal-then-offside events than shot or offside counts capture. We stress that this mechanism is a hypothesis, not a finding.

8.2 Threats to validity

  1. Outcome ascertainment. Events come from public match feeds, not from an official VAR log. If high-profile clubs' incidents are logged more diligently, counts are differentially inflated for Real Madrid. The cross-check with ESPN (Section 4.3) and the manual video review mitigate this but cannot eliminate it. Early seasons are less well corroborated by ESPN.
  2. Over-control. Offside flags are themselves produced by the same officiating process whose fairness is in question. Controlling for them (and for the offside-share null in test D) assumes the flags are unbiased; if they were biased the adjusted estimates would absorb part of any bias. The unadjusted 2.8× is the upper bound; the adjusted estimates should be read as conditional on offside flags. That said, the shots-based and xG-based adjustments do not use officiating outcomes and also leave a rate ratio of 1.9–2.2.
  3. Selection through the final table. “Top-10” is defined by the final table, i.e. conditional on a (partly) post-treatment outcome. It was requested to simplify comparisons; the full-league results are reported alongside and are consistent.
  4. Small counts and multiplicity. 50 events, nine seasons. Dispersion is modest (1.03–1.11), but p-values from many related tests are descriptive and not corrected for multiplicity. The main outlier result survives Bonferroni by a wide margin; the secondary descriptive cuts (timing, venue, opponent strength) do not and are reported as such.
  5. Dependence. Standard errors are clustered by match (both teams share a referee), not by team or team-season; team-level clustering would widen the intervals, although the conclusion that the Real Madrid ratio exceeds 1 is robust to that.
  6. Covariate quality. xG is a model output (Understat), PPDA is a zone-based proxy and foul-zone counts capture about 91% of fouls. PPDA terms are collinear.
  7. Officials. The recorded referee is the on-field referee, not the VAR official. Heterogeneity by VAR-room official could not be tested.
  8. Counterfactuals. Points that “would have been gained” assume the match otherwise unfolds identically. They ignore tactical and psychological knock-on effects, and scenario A assumes every nullification was wrong, which the accuracy literature makes implausible.
  9. Period and protocol effects. Season fixed effects absorb league-wide changes, but changes in VAR protocol, offside technology and coaching staff (Real Madrid had several head coaches in the period) are not modelled separately.
  10. Causal identification. The design is observational; absence of evidence for bias is not evidence of absence, particularly with 50 events.
  11. On-field dataset. It is phrase-based and a floor; goals ruled out with other wording are missed.
  12. Pipeline defects. A post-draft audit (Section 4.5, Appendix D) found five small defects (one non-VAR event counted as VAR, two weakly evidenced events, a foul-zone mis-merge, missing Understat aliases for two clubs and an abandoned match counted as 0–0). Their combined effect on any estimate is at most 0.05 on a rate ratio of about 2.0, but they illustrate that agreement between unofficial feeds does not rule out residual errors.

8.3 What would settle the question

  • Semi-automated or tracking-based offside data (the position of the offside line at the moment of the pass) to test the runners-in-behind hypothesis directly.
  • An official, event-level audit of every VAR intervention with a correct/incorrect label, which would give the error rate by beneficiary — the only direct test of favouritism.
  • All VAR checks (not only overturns), to estimate overturn rates per review by team.
  • The VAR-room official per match, to separate on-field and VAR-room effects.

9. Conclusions

  1. The post's direction is right, its numbers are not reproducible. Real Madrid leads La Liga in VAR-nullified goals (50 vs 30), confirmed in two independent providers, but the post's counts (42 for Real Madrid) are lower than any full reconstruction and no single definition reproduces all seven teams.
  2. The outlier is real (2.8× the rate of other teams; far outside the 99.8% funnel limit) and not an artefact of selection, clustering or data source.
  3. About half is explained by how much Real Madrid attacks and by the offsides that come with it; a residual of roughly 2.0× remains after controlling for shots, xG, offsides, pressing, fouls and cards.
  4. The residual does not look like targeting or favouritism. It is symmetric within matches, not concentrated in any referee, not specific to VAR (the same direction appears for on-field decisions), opponents are not spared, and VAR accuracy is too high for errors to produce it.
  5. The impact is modest. 44 matches contain a nullified goal; in 13 the result would have changed (21 points in 8.2 seasons). Counting every nullified goal as wrong would change one title (2020-21); under realistic error rates the probability is at most about 5%.
  6. Open question. The unexplained residual is most consistent with Real Madrid's attacking profile, a hypothesis that needs tracking data to confirm. We cannot exclude a modest VAR-specific effect (extra effect 1.36×, 95% CI 0.82–2.23), but we find no evidence of one.
In one sentence: Real Madrid is a statistical outlier whose excess is roughly half explained by how it attacks and whose remainder shows none of the fingerprints of unequal treatment; the data neither prove nor require a bias explanation.
